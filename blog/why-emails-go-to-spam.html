Why Your Emails Are Going to Spam (And the 7-Step Fix That Actually Works)

Most deliverability advice is vague. "Warm up your IP." "Clean your list." "Authenticate your domain." None of it tells you how to diagnose which specific issue is killing your inbox placement — or in what order to fix them. This is that guide.

Why Generic Deliverability Advice Fails

Every email platform has a help doc that says the same things. Authenticate your domain. Don't spam. Clean your list. Use a dedicated IP. It's all technically accurate and almost entirely useless when your emails are actually going to spam right now and you have campaigns to send.

The problem isn't the advice — it's that it treats deliverability as a checklist when it's actually a diagnostic process. Different deliverability failures have different root causes, and applying the wrong fix to the wrong problem at best does nothing and at worst makes things worse.

The core insight: Inbox placement is the output of a scoring model. ISPs (Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo) assign a sender score to every domain and IP. That score determines where your email lands. To fix placement, you need to understand which input to the model is depressing your score — and that requires diagnosis, not guesswork.

After doing this inside dozens of real DTC accounts, I can tell you that most brands have one or two specific issues dragging everything else down. Fix those, and placement restores quickly. Try to fix everything at once, and you end up making contradictory changes that confuse the ISPs and extend your problem by weeks.

The 4 Root Causes of Spam Filtering

At a systems level, there are four things that can cause your emails to land in spam — and only four. Everything else is a symptom of one of these:

1. Authentication failure

SPF, DKIM, or DMARC records are missing, misconfigured, or misaligned with your sending domain. This is the most common cause for brands that recently migrated platforms or set up Klaviyo without a proper technical onboarding. ISPs can't verify you are who you say you are, so they route your email to spam as a default risk-mitigation measure.

2. Reputation damage

Your sending domain or IP has accumulated negative signals — spam complaints, hard bounces, low engagement — that have degraded your sender score below the inbox threshold. This typically happens from buying lists, not suppressing disengaged subscribers, or a sending spike that looked like a spam attack to the ISP.

3. Content filtering

Your email content triggers keyword or pattern filters. This is less common than it was five years ago — modern ISPs are engagement-based, not keyword-based — but it still matters for certain industries (supplements, financial products, real estate) that have historically high spam rates.

4. Infrastructure misconfiguration

Your sending IP isn't warmed, you're on a shared IP with a damaged reputation, or your dedicated IP hasn't been configured correctly. Usually a platform/setup problem rather than a behavior problem.

~73%

of the deliverability audits I run on DTC accounts identify authentication or reputation issues as the primary cause. Content filtering and infrastructure problems account for the other 27% — but they're almost always secondary to one of the first two.

How to Diagnose Your Specific Problem

Before you fix anything, you need to know which of the four root causes applies to you. Here's the diagnosis sequence I use on every account:

Run an authentication check first

Go to emaildelivery.co and run your sending domain. This tells you whether SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are configured correctly, and whether they're aligned with each other. If any of these fail, that's your first fix — authentication issues have to be resolved before anything else will matter.

Check your sender reputation

Google Postmaster Tools shows your domain reputation (none, low, medium, high) as Gmail sees it. If you're sending to any volume and show as "low" or "none" on Gmail specifically, that's your problem. Microsoft SNDS does the same for Outlook/Hotmail recipients.

Audit your complaint rate

A spam complaint rate above 0.08% in Google Postmaster is the threshold where Gmail starts actively filtering you. Above 0.3%, you're in serious trouble. If your complaint rate is elevated, the root cause is almost always a list quality problem — recipients who never opted in, a very old list, or a segment that hasn't been engaged in 6+ months being treated like an active segment.

The 7-Step Fix Sequence

Once you've diagnosed your root cause, apply fixes in this order. The sequence matters — each step creates the conditions for the next one to work.

Step 1

Authentication Audit & Repair

Fix SPF, DKIM, and DMARC before touching anything else. Start with your DNS records. Your SPF record should include your sending platform (Klaviyo's SPF is include:klaviyo.com). DKIM should be a CNAME record pointing to Klaviyo's signing server. DMARC should be set to p=quarantine at minimum.

Time to impact: Authentication fixes propagate in 24–48 hours and show up in Google Postmaster within 3–5 days.

Step 2

Reputation Baseline

Check Google Postmaster Tools, Microsoft SNDS, and at least 2 blacklist checkers (MXToolbox covers most major blacklists). Document your current scores — you need a baseline to measure recovery against. If you're on any blacklists, submit for removal only after fixing the underlying cause (they'll just re-list you otherwise).

Step 3

Emergency List Hygiene

Before sending anything, suppress everyone who hasn't opened or clicked in the past 90 days. This sounds aggressive, but it's the only way to rapidly improve your engagement rate signal. A smaller, more engaged list sends better signals than a large, stale one. You can re-engage the suppressed segment later with a dedicated win-back sequence once your baseline is restored.

Step 4

Sending Volume Reduction

If your reputation is damaged, sending at full volume is like running on a broken ankle. Reduce to your most engaged segment (opened in the last 30 days) at 25–30% of your normal daily volume. This lets you send positive engagement signals without further damaging your reputation.

Step 5

Engagement-First Content

For the next 2–3 weeks, only send email where you're confident in the engagement rate. High-purchase-intent segments. Re-engagement bait with a real reason to click. Subject lines optimized for opens. Every positive engagement signal (open, click, reply, forward) is a vote in favor of your sender reputation.

Step 6

Gradual Volume Restoration

As your reputation scores improve, expand your sending segment slowly. Add back 30-to-60-day subscribers, then 60-to-90-day. Monitor complaint rate daily. The moment you see complaints tick above 0.05%, freeze and investigate before expanding further.

Step 7

Infrastructure & Monitoring Setup

Once you're back to healthy placement, set up ongoing monitoring so you catch problems early instead of after they've compounded. Google Postmaster alerts, a seed list for inbox placement testing, and a monthly authentication check take about 30 minutes per month to maintain.

💡 Typical recovery timeline: Authentication fixes: 3–5 days. Reputation restoration (after fixing root cause): 3–6 weeks. Full volume restoration: 6–8 weeks from starting the sequence. This feels slow, but trying to shortcut it by sending at full volume before your scores recover always extends the timeline.

What to Expect After Each Fix

Authentication fixes are the fastest-acting — you'll usually see movement in Google Postmaster within a week. Reputation recovery is slower because ISPs need to see a consistent pattern of good behavior before they revise their model.

  • Days 1–7: Authentication fixed, baseline scores documented
  • Days 7–14: Engagement rate climbing, complaint rate dropping
  • Days 14–28: Google Postmaster reputation moves from "low" to "medium"
  • Days 28–45: Volume restoration begins, inbox placement testing shows improvement
  • Days 45–60: Full volume restored, monitoring system in place

The brands I've worked through this process with consistently get back to 95%+ inbox placement within 60 days — even from very bad starting points. Vurbmoto went from 54% to 95% in 60 days following this exact sequence (you can read that full case study in the portfolio).

The key is discipline: don't try to rush the volume restoration, and don't start sending to large segments until your reputation scores confirm you're in the clear. The ISP algorithms are watching every send, and patience during recovery is what separates a 60-day fix from a 6-month slog.
